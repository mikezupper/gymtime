# Writing conventions

Write for the person who needs to act: an organizer setting up a season, a coach choosing time, a parent following a calendar, or a contributor changing the code. Identify that reader and the purpose before drafting. Use the conversation and requirements to resolve the brief; ask only when missing information would change the result.

Lead with the useful point. Explain concrete actions and their consequences in a sequence the reader can follow. Use the interface's exact labels in procedures and preserve exact commands, identifiers, dates, and units. Define unfamiliar concepts through an example, such as the difference between opening hours and published slots.

Keep essential instructions visible. Put optional detail in expandable help or a linked reference. Prefer plain words, active verbs, and connected paragraphs; use lists and tables when they make steps or comparisons easier to read. Avoid generic praise, slogans, and invented technical labels.

Preserve meaning when editing. Distinguish requirements, implementation choices, tested behavior, and proposals. Do not turn a local browser check into a claim about real users, production deployment, or calendar-provider refreshes. Explain actual limits where they affect a reader's decision.

Before finishing, check that the reader knows where to start, what to do, what should happen, and how to recover. Verify local links and commands. Keep decisions and operating instructions in repository documents, and personal data outside the public tree.
